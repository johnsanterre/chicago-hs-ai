3 · Read & play

The capstone, and the ladder

You have every piece: counting (1–2), the dial (3), context (4), learning (5), tokens (6), and the knowledge that data decides everything (7). The capstone assembles them:

Curate a corpus. Your own writing is the best choice — old essays, journals, messages you're comfortable using. Public-domain books are the other good option. The rule: your words, or words old enough to belong to everyone. Not your group chat (those aren't only your words), and not copyrighted text.

Train, tune, generate. Run the machine at two or three context lengths, find the temperature that suits your corpus, and generate a full page of output. Pick the best and worst passages.

Write the model card — the real product: what data went in (and what you chose to leave out), what the model does well, where it fails, and one thing it "believes" that is really a fact about your corpus.

And then place yourself honestly on the ladder. Your model has a few thousand numbers in it and read a few pages. GPT-2, which stunned the field in 2019, has 1.5 billion. The models you talk to daily are far past that — exact sizes undisclosed — trained on a large slice of everything ever written. Same bet-placing idea. Same falling loss. Same data-is-destiny rule. The distance between your machine and theirs is not a different kind of thing. It is this thing, scaled by a factor you can now count.

6 · Build

Ship it

Turn in the full capstone: the corpus description (not the corpus itself if it's personal), the generated page with best and worst passages marked, and the finished model card. Present three minutes to the class: one generated line, one thing your model learned about your voice, one thing it can never do.

Turn-in: page + model card + the three-minute presentation. The model card is the course grade.
