3 · Read & play

You are what you read

Everything your machine knows, it counted or learned from its corpus. Change the corpus and you change the machine — not its settings, its character. Train on formal essays and it writes long words and semicolon rhythms. Train on chat messages and it writes "lol" and drops its capital letters. Same code. Different diet.

This stops being cute and starts being important the moment you scale it up. The models you use were trained on a giant slice of the internet — which means their "voice," their assumptions, their blind spots, and their biases are the internet's, averaged. Course 1 told you that as a warning. You can now say it as an engineer: whatever is over-represented in the data is over-represented in the odds. A model is a mirror of its corpus, and nobody has ever built a corpus without a tilt.

It also means data quality is model quality. Feed your machine typos and it learns typos — faithfully. The best single upgrade you can give any model in this course isn't a smarter algorithm: it's better text.

And one more consequence, the personal one: a model trained on your writing picks up your voice — your sentence lengths, your favorite words, your habits. You'll meet that model in the capstone. Some students find it delightful. Most find it slightly unsettling. Both reactions are correct.
