Grounded or invented

3 · Read & play

The check that matters

Here is the uncomfortable truth at the center of every ask-your-documents system: the model's answer sounds exactly as good when the retrieval failed. Handed the right chunk, it writes a fluent, correct answer. Handed the wrong chunks — because the question used odd words, or the answer straddled two pieces, or it simply isn't in the pile — it writes a fluent answer anyway, assembled from whatever it got plus the shape of what such answers usually look like. The contract's refusal clause catches much of this, but not all: a chunk that is nearly relevant is the dangerous case, close enough that the model answers, wrong enough that the answer is subtly off.

So professionals run a grounding check: take each factual claim in the answer and find it in the provided chunks. Present — the claim is grounded; you can point at its support. Absent — it is invented, no matter how reasonable it sounds, and no matter that it might even be true. "True but ungrounded" still fails, because your system can't tell it from confident fiction — you'd be grading luck.

The check can be run by eye (the notebook trains you on five answers), and it can be run by a second model call: "here are the chunks and the answer; list every claim not supported by the chunks." A model checking text against text is on solid ground — that's reading, not remembering. If you took Research Agents, this is verification wearing a different shirt: same adversarial stance, same rule that support means surviving a hunt for the miss.

And one reframe to carry out of the lesson: when your system says "that's not in the provided documents" — that is a success. The pile has edges; a system that knows where they are is trustworthy at the edge, and one that papers over them is not trustworthy anywhere.
