3 · Read & play

Mirrors, manners, and your name

Bias. The model learned from what people wrote, and people's writing has lopsided defaults — which jobs get which pronouns, which neighborhoods get called "nice," whose food is "exotic." The model absorbed those patterns and reproduces them, statistically, without malice and without noticing. Training teams fight this with data filtering and behavioral tuning; it helps and doesn't finish the job.

Your working skill: notice the default, then push back. "Run it again with different assumptions" is a legitimate, professional prompt. If you're using AI output that involves people — characters, examples, hiring language — check who it cast by default. That check is your job, not the machine's.

Safety. The refusals you met in lesson 3 are the other half of this: the manners school tries to keep the tool from doing harm. It's imperfect in both directions — sometimes too cautious, sometimes not cautious enough. Neither surprises you now that you know how it was made.

Disclosure. When AI helped, say so. Not because using AI is shameful — you're in a course about using it well — but because your name on work is a promise about where it came from. Every school and workplace draws the line differently; know your school's line, follow it, and when in doubt use this test: if you'd be embarrassed for your teacher to see the chat log, you already know the answer.

The rule over all of it: you own what you make with the tool. Bias you didn't catch, errors you didn't check, credit you didn't give — all of it lands on the person whose name is on top.

4 · Try it

Tally the defaults

1. Pick a role: doctor, nurse, engineer, teacher, criminal, CEO.

2. In fresh chats, ask five times for "a short story about a ___" and tally: names, pronouns, ages, any details that repeat.

3. Now add: "vary your assumptions about who this person is." Compare the spread.

4. Trade tallies with a classmate who picked a different role. Five runs is a small sample — the real experiment is the whole class pooled: twenty students times five runs is a hundred stories. Build the class tally together.

If you find no pattern: that's the manners school actively fighting the mirror — modern models are heavily tuned on exactly the famous probes (doctor, nurse, engineer). The lopsidedness retreats to subtler ground: which neighborhoods get called safe, which names get picked for which roles, whose food is "exotic," which family structures appear by default. Probe there, pool the class results, and see what's left.

6 · Build

Your disclosure policy

Write your personal AI-use policy — half a page, first person, specific: when you'll use AI, when you won't, and when and how you'll say so. Include your school's actual rule (look it up or ask your teacher) and where your personal line sits relative to it.

Turn-in: the policy, plus your bias tally from the activity. You'll revisit the policy at the end of lesson 8 and see if it survived the course.
