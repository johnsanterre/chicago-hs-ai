3 · Read & play

The table that ate the alphabet

Your bigram machine has a one-letter memory: it knows the current letter and nothing before it. That's why its output dissolves — every letter is chosen as if the sentence just started.

The upgrade is obvious: remember more. Condition on the last TWO letters (a trigram model), or three, or four. Each extra letter of memory visibly improves the text: real words start surfacing, then short phrases. Run the numbers on the table, though:

1 letter of context: 27 rows. Two letters: 27 × 27 = 729 rows. Three: 19,683. Ten letters of context: about 206 trillion rows — and your corpus is a few thousand characters long. Almost every row would be empty. The model would mostly know nothing, very precisely.

This is the wall, and it's worth respecting because it stopped the whole field for decades: counting cannot scale to long context, because most long contexts have never occurred even once — anywhere. The sentence you're reading right now has never existed before, yet you understand it. A counting model cannot; it can only look up the past.

What breaks the wall is the subject of the next lesson: stop looking up contexts, start learning from them — so that similar-but-never-seen contexts share what the model knows.
