3 · Read & play

Nudge the numbers

Here's the move that broke the wall. Instead of a table of counts, give the model a grid of adjustable numbers — call them weights. To predict what follows t, the model combines its weights into betting odds. At the start the weights are random, so the odds are garbage.

Then the training loop, and it is almost insultingly simple:

Show it one real example from the corpus — "after t came h."
Score its bet: how much probability did it put on h? The score is a single number called the loss — big when the model was surprised by the truth, small when it expected it.
Nudge every weight a tiny step in whichever direction would have made the loss smaller.
Repeat — thousands of times.

No single nudge accomplishes much. All of them together drag the loss down, down, down — and a falling loss means the model's bets are getting sharper. When you train it in the notebook you will watch the number fall in real time, and that falling number is the same thing engineers at every AI lab stare at all day.

Why does this beat counting? A table can only look up contexts it has literally seen. Learned weights generalize: similar contexts share weights, so the model has sensible odds even for combinations it never met. That one property — plus a few thousand more of everything — is the road from your notebook to the models you talk to.
