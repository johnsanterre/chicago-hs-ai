3 · Read & play

Four levers

Everything the model knows about your situation is in your message. It can't see the assignment sheet, the rubric, or the three things you already tried. So the quality of the answer tracks the quality of the ask — almost mechanically.

Context. Who you are, what the situation is, who the audience is. "I'm a junior writing for an English teacher who cares about topic sentences" changes everything downstream.

Task. Say precisely what you want done. "Help me" is not a task. "Point out my three weakest sentences and explain why they're weak" is.

Format. Shape the output: a bulleted list, a table, three options, one paragraph, under 100 words. If you don't choose a shape, the model picks one — and its default is longer than anyone wants.

Limits. What it should not do. This is the lever people forget, and it's how you keep the AI a coach instead of a ghostwriter: "don't rewrite my work — critique it." Limits are where your integrity settings live.

Levers work in any order, and each one is a sentence you already know how to write. Notice which single lever moves the answer most for you.

One more habit, free: end hard prompts with "ask me two questions before you answer if anything is unclear." The model is good at knowing what it's missing — when you invite it to ask.

4 · Try it

The three-prompt experiment

Pick a real task from your actual life this week — an email, a study plan, a caption, a review. Run it three times in three separate chats:

Round 1: one vague sentence, the way most people ask.

Round 2: add real context (who you are, the situation, the audience).

Round 3: all four levers — context, exact task, output format, and at least one limit.

Put the three outputs side by side. The distance between round 1 and round 3 is the skill you just learned.
