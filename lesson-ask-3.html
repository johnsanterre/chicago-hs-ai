Search by words, and where it breaks

The first retriever is honest arithmetic: score chunks by the words they share with the question, weighting rare words most. It works — until the question and the answer use different words.

3 · Read & play

Counting shared words

Retrieval, first attempt: score every chunk by overlap with the question, return the top few. You can build it in an afternoon, and the whole thing is visible arithmetic.

Count shared words — "how many final-exam retakes do I get?" shares final, exam, retakes with §4.2, and almost nothing with the bus schedule. §4.2 wins.

Weight rare words most. Not all shared words are evidence. Sharing "the" with a chunk means nothing — every chunk has "the". Sharing "retake" means nearly everything — one section in the whole pile uses it. So each word's vote is scaled by its rarity across the pile: common words whisper, rare words shout. That one idea — rare-word weighting — powered real search engines for decades, and it is worth building by hand once, which the notebook does.

Now the break. Ask "when do we get out early on Wednesdays?" The answering chunk says "dismissal at 1:30 PM every Wednesday." Count the shared meaningful words: essentially none. Get out early and dismissal are the same idea wearing different words, and word arithmetic has no idea. The chunk scores near zero; retrieval returns the wrong pieces; the model downstream answers from noise or refuses. Notice what kind of failure this is: silent. No error appears — you just get worse answers, and unless you're measuring (lesson 7), you won't know. Words are not meanings. Getting from one to the other is lesson 4, and it's the best idea in this course.

6 · Build

Find the synonym holes

For every miss, diagnose it in one sentence: was the answer really absent, or present in different words? Keep this ten-question set — it becomes your measuring stick for the rest of the course.
