3 · Read & play

The assembly line

Ask a food pantry how many families they served last year and someone opens a drawer: monthly reports, some typed, some scanned, some in emails. The answer exists — spread across forty documents in forty formats. That drawer is where this course lives, because every school club, church, and nonprofit in Chicago has one.

The tempting move is one giant step: "computer, read all this and tell me the answer." Sometimes that even appears to work — and you have no idea whether to trust it, because you can't see where anything came from. The professional move is an assembly line, and it has the same stages every time:

Collect — gather the files, list what you have.
Extract — get the text out of each format.
Clean — fix the junk extraction leaves behind.
Structure — pull the fields you need into rows: date, amount, name.
Validate — check every row before trusting it.
Store — write a clean table.
Ask — now questions are cheap, and answers show their work.

Two properties make the staged version win. Each stage is small enough to check — you can look at its output and see whether it's right. And each stage is fixable alone — when page headers pollute your text, you fix the clean stage and rerun; nothing else changes. One giant step has neither property. That's the whole argument, and the figure lets you feel it.
