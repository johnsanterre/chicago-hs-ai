3 · Read & play

Greedy, warm, and molten

An obvious idea: skip the dice. Always pick the row's favorite — the highest-probability letter, every time. That's called greedy generation, and it fails in a way you can predict before running it: if e's favorite is space, and space's favorite is t, and t's favorite leads back to e — the machine writes "e the the the the" forever. Determinism plus a loop equals a stuck machine.

The fix is the dice you already have. But the dice have a dial: temperature. It reshapes the odds before you roll:

Cold (below 1): the favorites get MORE favored. At the extreme it becomes greedy — safe, repetitive, loop-prone.
Warm (exactly 1): the odds exactly as counted. Faithful to the data.
Hot (above 1): the odds flatten toward equal. Rare letters start winning rolls. Push far enough and it's static — every letter equally likely, all pattern gone.

Now you know why the big models answer the same question differently on different days, and why they have settings with names like "temperature" in their developer dashboards — you built the dial they're naming. There is a right temperature for a task: low for facts and code, higher for brainstorming. That intuition transfers up from your 27-letter machine to the trillion-parameter ones intact.
