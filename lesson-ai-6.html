3 · Read

Plausible is not true

Hallucination is the industry's word for confident invention: the model states something false with the same fluent tone it uses for facts. Fake studies. Misattributed quotes. Dates that are off by years. Court cases that were never filed — lawyers have been sanctioned for citing them.

It comes straight from the lesson-1 mechanism. The model writes plausible continuations. Truth and plausibility usually travel together — but when the fact is rare, recent, or just missing from training, the machine doesn't hit an empty cell in a database (there is no database). It keeps producing plausible text, because that's the only thing it does.

What makes this dangerous is that the wrongness has no flavor. Human lies have tells. A hallucination reads exactly like the truths around it — same confidence, same polish. You cannot detect it by tone. Ever.

So the rule, for the rest of your life with these tools: if it's checkable and it matters, check it. Names, quotes, numbers, citations, medical claims, legal claims — a minute of searching before you repeat it. You can also reduce (not eliminate) the risk: ask for verifiable sources, invite "I'm not sure," use tools with live search for recent facts. Reduction is not exemption. Verify.

4 · Try it

Set the trap

Pick a topic you genuinely know — your sport's records, a game's lore, a band's discography, Chicago history you're solid on.

1. Ask the AI ten detailed factual questions about it.

2. Grade every answer against sources or your own verified knowledge.

3. For anything wrong: ask the AI "are you sure?" and watch what happens — sometimes it corrects, sometimes it doubles down politely. Note which.

If all ten hold up: good — that's years of engineering against hallucination working, on a topic with plenty of training data. It is not a safety guarantee. Raise the stakes where hallucination still lives: ask for direct quotes with page numbers from a specific book, or five academic citations on a narrow topic, and verify those. The thinner the territory, the sooner the machine starts inventing.

6 · Build

The report card

Write the report card from your trap: the ten claims, your verdict on each (verified / wrong / couldn't check), sources for at least three verdicts, and — the headline — anything false you caught, quoted exactly.

Close with one sentence: what will you now always check before using AI output in real work?

Turn-in: the report card. Keep it; you'll cite your own evidence in class discussions.
