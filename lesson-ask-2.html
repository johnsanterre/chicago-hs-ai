3 · Read & play

The unit of retrieval

Store, retrieve, ask — and retrieval finds pieces. Before anything can be found, the pile has to be cut into them. Those pieces are called chunks, and a chunk is the atom of this whole system: it is what gets scored against your question, what gets retrieved, and what lands in front of the model. Cut badly and every later stage inherits it.

Too small, and meaning gets orphaned. Cut every two sentences and the retake policy shatters: the chunk that says "one retake per semester" no longer contains "for final exams" — that was two sentences up, in a different chunk. The retrieved piece is true and useless, because the condition and the rule live in separate atoms now.

Too big, and the answer gets buried. Cut by whole document and you're back to lesson 1's paste-everything problem in miniature: the chunk containing the answer is forty paragraphs of dress code with one relevant line inside. It scores poorly against the question (mostly noise), and if retrieved anyway, it spends your context on padding.

The working answer: cut on structure, not just length. Documents come with seams — headings, sections, paragraph breaks — and those seams are where meaning already separates. A few hundred words per chunk, aligned to sections where they exist, with a sentence or two of overlap at each cut so a rule and its condition never end up strangers. And every chunk keeps a receipt — which document, which section — because "the answer, and where it came from" is the shape of everything this course returns. If you took Research Agents, that's the same law that governed gathering.
