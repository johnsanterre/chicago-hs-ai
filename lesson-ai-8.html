3 · Read & play

It's a dial, not a switch

Most advice about AI in school has two settings: use it, or don't. That is why it lands as a rule someone handed you instead of a decision you make. The tool has four settings, and the skill in this lesson is choosing one — from what you already know about the machine, not from a list.

Do it for me. The AI produces the thing. You check it, then use it.
Coach me. You produce the thing. The AI explains, asks questions, points at the weak spot — and never writes the answer.
Check me. You already produced the thing. The AI hunts for mistakes in it.
Off. The AI stays out — and there is a mechanical reason why, not a moral one.

Where you set the dial follows from four facts about the machine. Three of them you learned earlier in this course. None of them are about you.

Fact 1 — the output is the machine's; the change is yours. Learning happens in your head while you do the hard part: pulling an idea out of memory, building an argument, getting a step wrong and finding out why. Psychologists call it the generation effect — you remember what you produced far better than what you read. So ask what the assignment is for. If it exists to produce a thing (a name for the robotics team, a poster, a script that renames files), the thing is the product and any setting works. If it exists to produce a change in you (the essay in a unit that is teaching persuasive writing, the problem set that builds the skill), the effort is the product. "Do it for me" hands in a document and skips the rep: the robot did your curls, the bar moved, you didn't. But Off is the wrong answer too. "Coach me" — draft it yourself, then where is my argument weakest? — is how writers have always gotten better. Only the top setting is out.

Fact 2 — there is no truth-checker inside. Lesson 6: the machine produces the most plausible next words. A wrong medicine dose comes out of the same process as a right one, with the same confidence, because confidence is part of the writing style it learned — not a signal about the world. So when a wrong answer costs something real and you can't check it, the setting is Off. Not because the machine is usually wrong, but because you cannot tell which time this is. "It sounded sure" is not a check. Two models agreeing is not a check; they read the same internet. The line is verifiability, not the topic: understanding the words on a lab report is checkable work; choosing a dose from it is not.

Fact 3 — the chat window is not on your device. What you paste travels to a company's servers. Most services store it; some train the next model on it unless you opt out; staff can read conversations that get flagged. None of that is sinister — it is how the product works — but it means a paste is a send. So before the paste, one question: whose information is this, and did they say yes? A friend's messages, the food pantry's donor list, your own passwords and medical history: Off, at least in a public chat window. The practicum courses teach the safe ways to do those jobs — redacted data, tools that run on your own machine.

Fact 4 — a message to a person carries a second message. When your friend reads your text on their worst day, the words say one thing, and the fact that you wrote them says another: you spent something on me. A thing means more when it cost the sender something. The machine's whole value is that it costs nothing — which is exactly why it cannot carry that second message. Researchers at Ohio State tested this: people told that a friend's supportive message had AI help rated the friendship lower and felt less sure where they stood. So apologies, condolences, the text to a friend who just got dumped: Off. Not because the machine writes badly — it writes fine — but because the point was never the words.

Look at what these are. Not rules about cheating. Not "AI bad." Four facts about what the machine is and isn't, and each one tells you which way to turn the dial. That is the difference between a user and an owner: a user follows the rules they were given; an owner understands the machine well enough to write their own.
