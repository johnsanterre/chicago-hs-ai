3 · Read & play

Ship it with its edges labeled

Everything assembles today, and the assembly starts with the questions that aren't technical.

Privacy, before anything runs. Whose documents are in your pile? If they mention real people — club rosters, meeting minutes with names — you need permission from whoever owns them, not just access. And know your system's data path: chunking and embedding run local in your notebook, but every question, and every retrieved chunk sent with it, goes to the model's provider at answer time. A pile you wouldn't paste into a chat is a pile that needs the sensitive parts removed before it goes behind an answering system. When in doubt, run a public pile.

Then the engineering, which you already own. Cut on seams with overlap. Embed and store. Rewrite queries if your users' phrasing needs it. Retrieve top-k, ask under the contract, ground-check what comes back. Every dial you set — chunk size, k, the rewriting step — now has a reason you can say out loud, and a number from your question set backing it.

The honest label. Real tools ship with their limits attached: what the pile covers and what it doesn't, the measured hit rate, and the standing instruction that a cited answer is checkable and an uncited one is a bug. If you took Research Agents, you've seen this move — the limits section is what separates a tool from a rumor machine. Same law here, and it's yours to enforce now.

Last thing, and it's the point of the whole course: find your user. A sibling who needs the team schedule, a club officer buried in old minutes, a parent facing a rulebook. Watch them ask real questions. Their third question — the one you never anticipated — will teach you more than any lesson on this site.

6 · Build

Ship the system

Your capstone ships four things:

1. The working notebook — your pile, your dials, ask() at the bottom, runnable top to bottom by a stranger.
2. The eval table — your ten questions (plus your user's new ones), graded: right, cited, grounded, refusals sorted into correct and retrieval-miss.
3. The limits label — what the pile covers, the measured hit rate, what the system must not be used for, and where questions go when asked.
4. The field report — who used it, their three most interesting questions, and what their questions broke.

Then the practicum move: leave the tool with its user, check back in a week, and write down whether they're still using it. That answer — either way — is real product feedback, and you earned it.
