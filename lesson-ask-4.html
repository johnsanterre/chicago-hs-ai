3 · Read & play

Where similar things are near

Lesson 3 died on a fact: words are not meanings. The repair is one of the best ideas in modern AI. An embedding model reads a piece of text and outputs a list of numbers — a vector, typically a few hundred numbers long — with one trained-in property: texts with similar meanings get vectors that are close together. "Dismissal at 1:30" and "when do we get out early" share almost no words, but they land near each other, because the model learned from billions of sentences that those phrasings live in the same contexts.

You can picture it as a map. Every chunk in your pile gets a point. The schedule chunks cluster in one region, the grading rules in another, the robotics minutes in a third — not because anyone sorted them, but because meaning determines position. A question becomes a point on the same map, and retrieval becomes geometry: return the chunks nearest the question. Closeness is measured with one small formula (cosine similarity — "do these vectors point the same way?"), and the notebook builds it in three lines.

So the pipeline is: embed every chunk once and store the vectors; embed each question as it arrives; rank chunks by nearness; return the top few. The embedding model runs free in your Colab — a small open-source one downloads in seconds, no API key — and your pile never leaves the notebook at this stage.

One honest note: the map isn't magic. Embeddings can put things near each other for shallow reasons (same names, same format), and a fact that isn't in your pile is still not in your pile — nearness can't invent the answer chunk. That's why lesson 7 measures instead of trusting. But the synonym hole — lesson 3's whole failure class — closes today.
