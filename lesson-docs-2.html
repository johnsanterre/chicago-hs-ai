3 · Read & play

Trapped text

Documents arrive in formats, and the formats form a ladder of difficulty. A .txt file or pasted email is already text — read it and go. A .csv is already structured — even better. A PDF looks like text but isn't: inside, it's drawing instructions — "place these characters at these coordinates" — and extraction must rebuild lines and paragraphs from character positions. It mostly works. Mostly.

A scan or photo is the hostile end: just pixels. Getting text out needs OCR — optical character recognition — which is a prediction system, and it makes prediction mistakes: l becomes 1, O becomes 0, and a coffee stain becomes a word.

So the rule that runs this whole course: extraction is lossy, and you inspect what survived before building on it. The classic casualties: page headers and footers interleaved mid-sentence, columns read in the wrong order, spacing collapsed or doubled, and characters silently swapped. None of these announce themselves. All of them poison every later stage if they get through — the structure stage will happily extract a donation amount of $1,847 from a document where OCR read $l,847.

In the notebook you'll build a PDF from raw bytes yourself — 700 bytes, and suddenly the format stops being magic — then extract your text back out of it and inspect the result.

6 · Build

The inspection report

Take two real documents from your lesson-1 pile (or any two PDFs you can legally use). Extract their text in the notebook and write the inspection report: what survived cleanly, what got damaged, and which of the classic casualties (headers, spacing, swapped characters, column order) you found.

Turn-in: the report. "Nothing was damaged" is a suspicious finding — look harder or say how you checked.
