One question becomes five searches

Real questions are too big for one search. A research agent decomposes the question into angles — separate searches that each cover a different part — because coverage, not luck, is what finds the answer.

3 · Read & play

Coverage beats luck

Type one search for "is the Riverside garden growing?" and you get whatever that one query happens to hit. Maybe the news story. Maybe the garden's own page. Whatever it is, you have one keyhole view — and no way to know what you missed, because missing things is silent.

A research agent's first real move is not searching. It is decomposition: turning the question into 4–6 angles, each one a separate search aimed at a different part of the answer. For the garden question:

Plot history — has the number of plots changed over the years?
Membership — are more families joining or leaving?
Funding — is money coming in — grants, city support?
Coverage — is anyone outside the garden writing about it?

Each angle is a small bet about where evidence might live, and together they cover the question the way one query never can. This is also the first place the LLM earns its seat in a research agent: writing good angles takes reading the question for what it actually asks, and that is meaning-work, the kind rules can't do — the same boundary you saw if you took Document Pipelines. In the notebook the model proposes the angles; you count what each one reaches.

One honest caution before the figure: more angles is not free. Every angle costs searches, fetches, and reading time, and past a point new angles just re-find the same pages. The skill is enough angles to cover, few enough to afford — professionals usually land near five.

6 · Build

Decompose two questions

Take two real questions you actually care about — one local ("should our school start later?"), one bigger. For each, write 4–6 angles by hand, each with one sentence on where you expect that evidence to live. Then run the notebook's decomposer on the same questions and compare: which angles did the model find that you missed, and which of yours beat the model's?

Turn-in: both angle sets, yours and the model's, with your comparison sentences.
